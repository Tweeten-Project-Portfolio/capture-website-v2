FAQs

Plastic (and a world filled with plastic waste) is a very convoluted subject. Here are a few answers to common questions that may inspire you to dive deeper into the pollution crisis.

Short answer: it is not getting recycled. ~95% of the time, our plastic ends up incinerated, in landfills, or leaked into our environment (including our bodies!).



Longer answer: Plastic is a very unique branch of materials. There are hundreds of thousands of different types of plastic, each with their own chemical makeup and properties. In addition, plastic also often contains all sorts of dyes, stabilizers, and other additives to make it better suited for its intended use. This is a fantastic feature when it comes to fitting the needs of your product.

However, this variability is plastics' greatest curse.

When it comes to recycling, all of these countless types of plastic become extremely difficult to properly sort and process. For example, the current recycling identification system in the United States recognizes only 7 different types of plastic:

#1 - PET (Polyethylene Terephthalate) - most often used in water bottles,
#2 - HDPE (High-Density Polyethylene) - milk jugs, detergent bottles,
#3 - PVC (Polyvinyl Chloride) - pipes, credit cards,
#4 - LDPE (Low-Density Polyethylene) - plastic bags, six-pack rings, flexible container lids,
#5 - PP (Polypropylene) - yogurt containers, straws, bottle caps,
#6 - PS (Polystyrene) - disposable coffee cups, plastic food boxes, plastic cutlery,

AND, finally,


#7 - "Other" plastics.


What classifies a plastic as being a #7 category? Basically, every one of the other hundreds of thousands of plastic configurations in existence that is NOT one of the first six plastic types!

When there are so many different configurations of plastic and so few ways to distinguish them, this becomes a massive logistics problem. Let's walk through a hypothetical scenario:

Imagine all of these different types of plastic we know and use as household liquids. Usually, we just use the product on its own, in the way we purchased it; we don't often mix any of the liquids together, unless we know that they will make a good duo (coffee and creamer, for example). Otherwise, we can end up mixing two items that CAN NEVER or SHOULD NEVER be mixed at all (oil and water, milk and coke, bleach and ammonia... to name a few!). Now, to make matters things all the more confusing, all of the liquids we have in the house are all in glass jars with no labels on them, except for a handful that get used fairly often (#1 is water, #2 is soap, #3 is orange juice, etc.). Apart from the few liquids with numbers on them, you have no foolproof way to know what is in each jar. We can use some of our senses and tools to figure it out (if something smells like vinegar, it probably is vinegar), but there is no tried and true method that works for discerning what each jar contains. What do we do in this mess?

Sadly, this scenario reflects the current state of plastic recycling. Other than finding the number inside the triangle on the plastic container, there really is no easy or accurate way to know what type of plastic you have. Plus, we haven't even discussed the idea that different additives can make recycling plastic of the SAME NUMBER hard to achieve!

"Now, surely if we put our plastic in the recyclables container, there must be some way for the landfill or recycling facility to sort it all out, right?"

Not entirely... From the minute the plastic enters the magic recycling bin, there are all sorts of ways the process can go wrong.

First: Pickup. Many cities and communities have recycling pickup services, but you may be shocked to learn that a large portion of these services do not take them to a recycling facility. Instead, the plastic gets sent straight to the landfill, where it will sit for hundreds (or thousands!) of years. Why does this happen? Often, the number of nearby facilities that can actually process plastic is very limited. In North Central Iowa, the nearest residential Material Recovery Facility (MRF) is in Lakeville, MN - nearly 2 hours away!

  • Sort your plastic by type prior to drop-off/shipment.
  • Once sorted, separate your plastic by color (clear vs colored).

We've all heard the adage "Reduce, Reuse, Recycle." This phrase is a good starting point to tackle pollution, but it's even more important to consider the order of the phrase. In fact, there's a more effective diagram to demonstrate this concept (more on that later!)

So - why is "Reduce" the first word? Because it is the most effective way to prevent and minimize pollution, full stop. This is the core driving force of sustainability in an economy of overconsumption. If there is one thing you can take home from this page, let it be this:

The most sustainable purchase is the one you never have to make.


Let's say that again.

The most sustainable purchase is the one you never have to make!


Reduction is the holy grail to making our lives more sustainable. In a world of finite resources, we MUST be concious as to how much we consume and dispose of. It is not a matter of if we will run out of resources, but when.

If this is the case, what does it look like to reduce?

  • Before you make a purchase, ask youself: "Do I really need this?" - This is not to say that we should never buy anything for our enjoyment or comfort, but when we do buy, we should consider how long we will use the item, if the item serves a purpose you can't already accomplish via other items on hand, if the item can be repurposed or easily recycled, and if the item produces other waste during its lifecycle of use.
  • Refuse the "slop" - Whether it's the freebie items that get handed out at events that you'll never end up using, the single use plastic utensils at a grillout, or the plastic bags from the grocery store, learning how to live without taking these items is an extremely easy way to start reducing your waste footprint.
  • Advocate for change - You don't have to go straight to the White House to make a difference! Even if it's mentioning an idea or two about how to reduce waste to your family, friends, or local businiesses and city councils, you may be surprised at how many people are willing to learn and adopt these practices.
Next in the order is "Reuse." If you can't find a way to reduce your consumption for a certain application, reusing is the next best thing. Buying secondhand items is not only economically concious, but it is also extremely environmentally friendly! Or, if you have something that no longer works for its original purpose, can you find a way to utilize it for something else? For example, if your bath towels have frayed to the point where it no longer makes sense to use, how about cutting them up to make rags for cleaning up messes? If you find that you cannot refuse, reduce, or reuse, ONLY THEN should recycling be considered as the last resort. Waste hierarchy diagrams do a fantastic job of putting this concept into one neat picture. Take a look at the diagram below, from Waste Nothing of New Zealand, that illustrates the importance of "Reducing, Reusing, and Recycling" (in that order!).

We would love to be able to take all the plastic that we can; unfortunately, however, not all plastics process equally.

As mentioned in the answer to What is happening to our plastic waste?, we only designate 7 categories of plastic in the United States. Therefore, any plastic that is labeled as #7 is unusable for us to process, unless we know exactly what type of plastic it is and we have enough volume of that specific plastic to afford to process it.

Looking closer into the 6 remaining categories of plastic, we will also eliminate #3 (PVC) and #6 (PS) from our accepted plastics. These two plastics are either too tough to process or too dangerous to process without a specialized facility, which would cost several million dollars to construct. So, for the time being, we accept #1, #2, #4, and #5 plastics for recycling purposes. #7 plastics will only be accepted IF we know what kind of plastic it is, there is enough volume to justify the processing cost, and that we know it is safe to process within our workshop.

If you'd like to dig a little bit into the science of plastic compatability, feel free to read on!

Zooming into molecular level, plastics are made up of very long chains of carbon atoms with all sorts of other atoms (or functional groups) attached to them. These functional groups give a specific plastic its unique properties, depending on the type of atom or molecule.

For example, when fluorine is attached to a carbon chain to make PTFE (or Teflon), it allows the plastic to be chemically inert, resistant to heat, and non-stick. These characteristics make it appealing to use Teflon for cookware; HOWEVER, as we are learning more about PFAS and other "forever chemicals," and the effects they have on our endocrine system and the external environment, it is becoming more and more apparent that Teflon is not a plastic we want to be using in everyday life, nor in our products.

Or, when chlorine is substituted into a carbon chain to make PVC, it allows the plastic to be very rigid and durable. This makes it appealing to use PVC for pipes and other construction materials; HOWEVER, if PVC is burned or incinerated, it can release highly-dangerous hydrogen chloride gas and other toxic dioxins into the air. Icky.

Just taking a look at these two examples, we can see that the different atoms attached to the carbon backbone allow for ALL sorts of material properties and applications. This is great for when products need to be fine-tuned for a specific appllication, but for us recyclers, all the variation means that we have to be very careful about what plastic we accept. If we mix different types of plastic together, we may either find ourselves with a product that is unusable or undesirable, or we may open up the possibility of risking the health of our employees if the plastic burns or produces fumes.

With a labor of love! ...And labor. Here are a few steps to take your trash and turn it into treasure!
  • CaptuRE takes plastic from local businesses, households, and municipalities.
  • Depending on the quality and cleanliness of the plastic waste, some further steps may be required before we can use it. This may include spraying with soap and water, using a heat gun to remove labels and adhesives, or cutting off lids and other plastic components that are not the main body of the plastic container.
  • Once the plastic is clean and free of any other incompatiable plastics/contaminants, we may begin the recycling process!
  • We use two processing machines designed by the open-source project Precious Plastic, a global foundation that empowers everyday people to make a living cleaning the plastic waste scattered around our Earth. Precious Plastic provides blueprints for all sorts of machines, molds, and designs, but the two machines we use are the shredder and the sheet press.
